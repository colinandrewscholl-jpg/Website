About Colin

Colin Scholl is a Ph.D. Candidate in Experimental Psychology at the University of Texas at El Paso (UTEP), specializing in psycholinguistics, cognitive psychology, and discourse processing.

His primary research examines how readers construct, update, and maintain mental representations (situation models) when reading passages containing evolving character behaviors or unexpected event sequences. By comparing behavioral paradigms with millisecond-accurate eye-tracking metrics, Colin identifies the precise temporal dynamics of memory updating and cognitive conflict resolution.

In addition to discourse comprehension, Colin leads empirical projects investigating sentence-level language switching in Spanish-English bilinguals in the El Paso / Juárez border region, evaluating how executive control mechanisms adapt to high-frequency code-switching environments.

Theoretical Frameworks

Discourse Comprehension: The C-I Model & The Landscape Model

Research in discourse comprehension seeks to explain how readers construct coherent mental representations of a written text, known as situation models. There are two key discourse processing frameworks:

1. The Construction-Integration (C-I) Model

Proposed by Kintsch (1988; 1998), the C-I model posits that comprehension occurs in two distinct phases. First, the Construction phase, where the reader generates a mental network of the information from a discourse (propositions) directly from the text's exact wording (surface form) and meaning (textbase) via a bottom-up, associative process that contextually irrelevant information can obstruct. Second, the Integration phase operates via network activation across propositions and the reader's background knowledge, strengthening context-consistent nodes and inhibiting irrelevant ones within the mental model. This framework explains why unexpected narrative shifts or inconsistent character traits (e.g., a "vegan" character ordering a hamburger) incur immediate processing costs during reading (Scholl & Schwartz, 2026).

2. The Landscape Model

Developed by van den Broek and colleagues (1999), the Landscape Model shifts the focus of discourse processing onto the dynamic cognitive activation that occurs during reading. This model conceptualizes comprehension as a landscape where cyclical reading processes activate concepts based on coherence relations (causality, intentionality, space, and time). Therefore, highly connected causal nodes receive sustained activation and are easily retrieved from memory, whereas unlinked or poorly integrated episodic events fade rapidly. This model predicts differential eye-movement reading patterns (e.g., regressions and increased total viewing times) when readers must update their situation models.

Theoretical Frameworks

Bilingual Language Control: The BIA+ Model & The Adaptive Control Hypothesis

Bilingual language processing requires constant management of multiple active lexical systems to minimize processing errors. Two core frameworks guide my investigations into how bilinguals navigate this unique cognitive challenge:

1. The Bilingual Interactive Activation+ (BIA+) Model

Formulated by Dijkstra and van Heuven (2002), the BIA+ model accounts for non-selective lexical access during visual word recognition. It separates a word recognition system (sub-lexical, lexical, and orthographic representations across languages interacting via bottom-up and lateral inhibition) from a task/decision system (responsible for task demands, explicit response strategies, and socio-cultural context). Crucially, the BIA+ model dictates that bilingual language activation is largely stimulus-driven and impervious to top-down voluntary control over lexical access itself, though task demands modulate output behaviors.

2. The Adaptive Control Hypothesis (ACH)

Proposed by Green and colleagues (Abutalebi & Green, 2008; Green & Abutalebi, 2013), this framework addresses how bilinguals adapt their executive control network based on habitual language interaction contexts (e.g., single-language environments versus dense code-switching communities like the El Paso–Juárez border). The ACH posits that different interactional contexts engage distinct sub-components of cognitive control—such as goal maintenance, task switching, selective inhibition, and monitoring, which ultimately reshapes both domain-general and linguistic executive functioning.

Analytical Frameworks

Quantitative Methods: Linear Mixed-Effects & Bayesian Modeling

Psycholinguistic data, particularly eye-tracking fixations and discourse comprehension accuracy, present complex hierarchical structures with crossed random effects of participants and items. I utilize advanced statistical techniques to ensure robust, reproducible inferences about my data:

1. Linear Mixed-Effects Models (LMMs) & Generalized LMMs

Standard ANOVA approaches often fail to simultaneously account for subject and item variability, leading to inflated Type I error rates (Baayen et al., 2008). Using R (lme4 and lmerTest packages), I fit maximal random-effects structures to estimate fixed experimental manipulations while controlling for individual baseline reading proficiency and passage-specific characteristics.

2. Bayesian Cumulative Probit & Credible Intervals

For metacognitive confidence ratings of comprehension, accuracy, and eye-movement fixation durations, I implement Bayesian regression models using Stan (via the brms package). Bayesian frameworks provide direct probabilistic interpretations of parameter estimates via Posterior Credible Intervals, avoiding dichotomous p-value thresholds and allowing the integration of informed priors based on prior psycholinguistic literature.

2. R Studio Workshop

Over Summer 2026, I co-hosted a 5-session R Studio workshop with Jayce Owens-Boone M.A. to teach coding in R to the UTEP Psychology department faculty and staff.

Methodological Paradigms

Neurocognitive Methods: Eye-Tracking, EEG & Pupillometry

To capture the real-time temporal dynamics of reading comprehension and language control, I employ high-precision neurocognitive methods:

1. Eye-Tracking During Reading (EyeLink 1000 Plus)

Eye-tracking provides an ecologically valid window into cognitive processing (Rayner, 1998). I measure early-stage measures (first fixation duration, gaze duration) reflecting lexical access and word recognition difficulty, alongside late-stage measures (go-past time, total reading time, regression path duration) reflecting semantic integration difficulties, repair processes, and situation model updating.

2. Electroencephalography (EEG) Event-Related Potentials (ERPs)

Complementing behavioral tracking, EEG records millisecond-resolution electrical brain activity. I analyze specific ERP components such as the N2 (sensitive to conflict and expectancy violations) and the P300b (indexing unexpected language membership categorization for code-switched sentences), to assess the comprehensiveness of bilingual word recognition models.

3. Pupillometry & Task-Evoked Pupillary Responses (TEPR)

In addition to eye-tracking, pupillometry captures attentional allocation differences in language comprehension via autonomic nervous system modulation. Task-Evoked Pupillary Responses (TEPRs), driven primarily by locus coeruleus-norepinephrine (LC-NE) system activation, serve as a sensitive index of cognitive effort, processing load, and surprisal during word recognition and situation model updating (Kahneman, 1973; Beatty, 1982; Winn et al., 2018). In the future, I aim to quantify the immediate attentional and inhibitory demands imposed by bilingual sentence processing by measuring pupil dilations time-locked to code-switched critical words.
